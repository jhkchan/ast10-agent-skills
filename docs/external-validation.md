# External validation — the detectors over 360 skills that never heard of them

The generated blocks below come from `eval/external-validation.json` via
`python3 scripts/external_validation.py`. Do not hand-edit them: run the
generator. The prose is written by hand and is the part that argues.

Every other measurement this repository publishes is taken over fixtures it
wrote itself. `docs/f1-report.md` says so on its own face — a perfect F1 over a
hand-authored corpus "says the rule separates the corpus it was built against"
and nothing more. This document is the first measurement taken over input the
repository did not author, did not curate, and could not have anticipated: 360
skill packages written before these detectors existed, by people who have never
read them.

It is a **false-positive study and nothing else.** Read the last section before
quoting any number from the earlier ones.

## What it cost

The corpus falsified a published assumption and produced three false positives
in checks that count toward a published F1 — two of them now fixed, one still
open.

1. **F1 = 1.000 was measured in a world the wild does not resemble.** 50 of the
   64 labeled fixture cases declare a `permissions` block. 0 of the 360 real
   packages do. Every check whose logic reads a manifest was therefore
   scored exclusively on packages that have one, and behaves differently on
   packages that do not — which is all of them.
2. **One check that counts toward AST01's F1 had a false-positive mode the
   fixture corpus was structurally incapable of exposing.** It fired on two real
   packages, and both firings were wrong. Fixed; see
   [The covering check that broke](#the-covering-check-that-broke).
3. **A false positive in a second covering check is still open.** It fired on one
   package in the only genuinely third-party corpus here, it is faithful to its
   registry scenario, and it is not fixed. See
   [The one still open](#the-one-still-open). It is listed rather than quietly
   repaired because a validation report that only contains resolved problems is
   an advertisement.

What it did **not** cost: no published figure moved. The two fixes changed no
number in `eval/f1-report.json`, `docs/f1-report.md`,
`docs/skill-judge-dashboard.md`, or the README badge row — all three generators'
`--check` modes pass unchanged, and the F1 JSON is byte-identical before and
after. That was the thing to verify rather than assume, and the reason it holds
is [below](#why-no-published-number-moved).

## The headline: fixtures declare, the wild does not

Not one of the 360 packages ships a `skill.usf.yaml`. All 360 have readable
`SKILL.md` frontmatter, and not one of them uses it to declare a permission or a
content hash. USF v1 is a proposal in a whitepaper; nothing in the world
implements it yet, and a corpus of real skills is a corpus of packages that
declare nothing.

**Only 43 of the 360 packages were written by someone unconnected to this
repository.** Corpus B and corpus D — 287 packages, 80% of the total — are the
maintainer's own work in other projects. Corpus C is the same organisation, a
different author. That is a real weakness in the design and it is stated here
rather than in a footnote: "third-party" in this document means *written without
knowledge of these detectors*, which every package satisfies, and does **not**
mean *independently authored*, which 317 of them do not.

Two further hygiene notes, both of which inflate the package count and neither
of which was corrected, because correcting a corpus after seeing its results is
how a false-positive rate gets talked downwards:

- Corpus D is defined mechanically as every directory containing a `SKILL.md`, so
  its 87 are 59 shipped skills, 17 in-repo test fixtures, 10 installed copies
  under a `.claude/skills/` directory, and 1 vendored third-party package.
- 8 of the 360 packages are byte-identical duplicates of another one in the set
  (352 distinct `SKILL.md` digests among 360 packages): 7 are paired test
  fixtures inside one repository, and 1 is a third-party skill present in both
  corpus A and corpus D. Two more are near-duplicates rather than exact ones —
  one skill republished under a different name — and they account for two of the
  seven adjudicated firings below.

## Three checks fired on 360 of 360 — and the contract already knew

Three checks fire on every package in both corpora, before and after the fixes:

| Check | `covers` | Evidence, on all 360 |
| --- | --- | --- |
| `AST01-content-hash-missing` | `artifact-signal-only` | `manifest.content_hash.value is unset` |
| `AST03-unbounded-write-scope` | `category-precondition` | `no permissions block at all: the package declares no files/network/shell scope` |
| `AST06-missing-sandbox-declaration` | `artifact-signal-only` | `manifest.permissions is unset or empty` |

They are not detecting a vulnerability. They are detecting **the absence of a USF
manifest**, which is the normal condition of every published skill.

The result worth stating is what did *not* go wrong. All three were already
ruled non-covering — `artifact-signal-only` or `category-precondition` — by
`CHECK_COVERAGE` in their own modules, long before anyone ran them over a real
corpus. None of them contributes to any published F1: `eval/generate_f1_report.py`
computes the gate verdict from the `covers: full` block alone and scores the
proxies into a separate `artifact_signal_only` block that never enters it. So the
1,080 firings those three contributed to this corpus inflated nothing.

That is the per-scenario detectability contract (ADR-0004) doing exactly the job
it was written for, on a case it had never seen. It is the strongest positive
result in this document, and it is a design result rather than a detection one:
**the repository classified these checks correctly before it had any evidence
about how they behave in the wild.**

The remaining defect was presentational, and it was real — see
[The word that misled](#the-word-that-misled).

## The covering check that broke

`AST01-undeclared-egress` declares `covers: full`. It decides AST01-S10 and it
counts toward AST01's published F1 of 1.000. Over the pre-fix tree it fired on
two of the 360 packages. Both firings were wrong, and identically so:

```
scripts/judge_noise_floor.py: egress call (requests.get() to host(s) ['localhost'] absent from the declared allowlist []
```

The source is `requests.get(args.url, ...)` where `--url` defaults to
`http://localhost:11434/api/chat` — a local model server's API. Nothing leaves
the machine. The second firing was the same shape against a public dataset API
the skill exists to read:

```
scripts/inspect_hf_dataset.py: egress call (httpx.get() to host(s) ['datasets-server.huggingface.co'] absent from the declared allowlist []
```

The allowlist prints as `[]` in both, and that is the whole defect. The scenario
is a *diff* — a destination the code sends to that the manifest did not promise.
With no manifest there is no promise, so the check had nothing to diff against
and degenerated into **"makes a network call and declares nothing"** — which is
true of every real skill that calls anything at all.

The fix makes the check refuse to convict on another category's precondition,
and excludes loopback destinations before the allowlist is consulted at all. Both
negatives now say which one happened:

```
precondition, not this scenario: the package declares no egress policy at all
(permissions.network is absent, which is not the same as an allowlist declared
empty), so there is no declaration for a destination to depart from. That absence
is AST06-missing-sandbox-declaration's and AST03-unbounded-write-scope's finding.
Left unevaluated here: scripts/judge_noise_floor.py: requests.get( to ['localhost']
```

The genuine scenario still convicts: a manifest declaring one host beside a
script posting to another is unchanged, which is what
`skills/AST01/scripts/test_ast01_detector.py` holds.

### Why no published number moved

Because the branch the fix changed is the branch no fixture can reach. All 16 of
AST01's labeled cases declare `permissions.network`, so every one of them enters
the *comparison* path, which the fix left alone. `eval/f1-report.json` is
byte-identical before and after; `eval/generate_f1_report.py --check`,
`scripts/generate_badges.py --check` and `eval/generate_dashboard.py --check` all
pass against the committed artifacts. **No published figure changed, and this
paragraph exists because that had to be verified rather than assumed.**

## The one still open

`AST08-S04` (Context-Dependent Malice) declares `covers: full` and fired on one
package in corpus A — the only genuinely third-party corpus here:

```
evals/setup_test_env.py:40: branch keyed to path.exists guards a call to shutil.rmtree() --
an environment-keyed guard wrapping a dangerous branch executes only where the attacker
chooses, so a test environment sees the safe path
```

The source is:

```python
# Clean if exists
if path.exists():
    shutil.rmtree(path)
```

That is the most common cleanup idiom in Python, inside a script whose job is to
build a throwaway test project. It is a false positive.

It is **not** a drift between the check and its scenario. `scenarios/registry.yaml`
names "file-existence probe" as an environment-identity guard, and the detector
implements exactly that. The imprecision is in the scenario definition: the guard
list admits `if exists → remove`, and that pattern is far more often hygiene than
malice. Narrowing it — requiring the guarded branch to be reached *only* under the
probe, or excluding a path the same function created — is a change to a covering
check and therefore a change to what AST01…AST10's F1 measures, so it is filed
here as a finding rather than made quietly at the end of a validation run.

After the fixes, this is the only firing in 7,920 covering-check runs.

## The word that misled

`AST04-invisible-unicode-smuggling` fired on three packages. One is a
linguistics skill whose `SKILL.md` documents zero-width joiners:

| Script | ZWJ / ZWNJ usage |
| --- | --- |
| Arabic | ZWNJ separates connected forms (e.g., Persian "mi&lt;ZWNJ&gt;khaham") |
| Emoji (modern) | ZWJ joins sequences (e.g., a family &lt;ZWJ&gt; sequence) |

The joiners are written out by name here; in the skill itself they are the live
code points, because the table is teaching what they do. U+200C and U+200D are
the subject matter. AST04's own `SKILL.md` already had the
doctrine right — invisible code points are "flagged as a carrier class and stopped
there, not convicted as an instruction" — and `CHECK_COVERAGE` already ruled the
check `category-precondition`, deciding no named scenario. **The logic was never
wrong. The report was.** Every row printed the same word:

```
AST04   Insecure Metadata                 6 check(s), 1 detected  [scope: all-files]
  DETECTED  AST04-invisible-unicode-smuggling         static-detectable
            SKILL.md: 3 invisible code point(s) ['U+200C', 'U+200D']
...
Summary: 36 check(s) over 10 categories, 4 detected; 2 categories ship no static detector.
```

`4 detected` on a package where nothing was detected at all. The audit renderer
now carries each check's `covers` ruling out to the operator, one word per row —
`FINDING`, `signal`, `observed`, `clear` — and states the verdict in a sentence:

```
AST04   Insecure Metadata                 6 check(s): 0 findings, 0 signals, 1 observed property  [scope: all-files]
  observed  AST04-invisible-unicode-smuggling         decides no named scenario
            SKILL.md: 3 invisible code point(s) ['U+200C', 'U+200D']
...
Summary: 36 check(s) over 10 categories; 2 categories ship no static detector.
    0 findings            decides a named scenario, and fired
    2 signals             an enabling precondition beside a scenario — never coverage of it
    2 observed properties a property of the package; decides no named scenario at all
Nothing in this audit decides a named attack scenario.
```

A reader who stops at the summary now comes away with a true impression. Over
this corpus that sentence is the correct one for 359 of the 360 packages.

## What this does not establish

**This measures the false-positive rate on real input and finds it low. It says
nothing whatever about recall in the wild.**

There are no labelled real-world positives in these corpora. All 360 packages are
presumed benign — not verified benign, *presumed*, on the strength of their
provenance and the fact that nothing in them looked wrong. Not one of them is a
known-malicious skill. So this document contains no true positives, no false
negatives, and therefore no recall, no precision and no F1 over real input. The
only quantity it measures is how often the detectors fire on benign work.

**The repository cannot claim it would catch a real malicious skill.** Nothing
here is evidence that it would. The published per-category F1 of 1.000 is measured
over fixtures this repository's own authors wrote to exercise its own rules, and
this validation does not extend it — it narrows it, by showing that one of those
rules behaved differently the first time it met a package nobody had written for
it.

Three further limits, stated plainly:

- **80% of the corpus shares an author with this repository.** See
  [The corpora](#the-corpora). A false-positive rate measured largely on one
  person's house style is a weaker result than the package count suggests.
- **A benign corpus cannot exercise most of the suite.** 29 of the 36 checks
  never fired in either run. Their behaviour on real input is untested in both
  directions, and no amount of further benign input would change that.
- **The counts are a dated measurement, not a live one.** They were taken on
  the date in `eval/external-validation.json`, against the tree named there. The
  corpora are not in this repository and cannot be in CI.

### What would fix it

Labelled real-world positives. Specifically, and in rough order of value:

1. **A skill from a documented supply-chain campaign** — a package with a public
   write-up establishing what it did, so a firing can be scored against a known
   ground truth rather than a reader's judgement.
2. **A registry takedown artifact** — a skill pulled from a public marketplace
   for cause, which gives a positive label with an authority behind it.
3. **A real typosquat** — a package published under a name confusable with a
   popular skill, which is the one AST-category attack that leaves an artifact a
   static check can see and that no synthetic fixture reproduces honestly.

Ten such packages, correctly labelled, would say more about this repository's
detectors than another thousand benign ones. Until they exist, every recall claim
in this repository is a claim about fixtures, and this document is a claim about
false positives.

## Reproducing this

The corpora live in eight checkouts outside this repository and are not vendored
— licence, size, and the point of the exercise is that they are other people's
work. A re-run needs them present. Given a `corpus.json` of
`{"label", "path"}` pairs:

```sh
# 1. audit the same packages with each tree, through the same harness
git archive <pre-fix-commit> | tar -x -C /tmp/before-tree
python3 scripts/external_validation.py --audit corpus.json --tree /tmp/before-tree --out before.json
python3 scripts/external_validation.py --audit corpus.json --out after.json

# 2. fold both dumps into the committed record
python3 scripts/external_validation.py --record --before before.json --after after.json \
    --before-tree <pre-fix-commit> --after-tree <post-fix-commit> --date <YYYY-MM-DD>

# 3. rewrite this file's generated regions
python3 scripts/external_validation.py
```

`corpus.json` is deliberately not committed: it holds local absolute paths, and
naming those checkouts is what the opaque corpus labels exist to avoid.

## How these numbers are kept honest

`eval/external-validation.json` is the machine-readable record, and
`scripts/external_validation.py --check` fails if this file's generated regions
and that record part company — the same contract `scripts/generate_badges.py`
holds over the README badge row.

Because the corpus cannot be in CI, `tests/test_external_validation.py` guards
the half that can be. It re-derives the entire 36-check roster from the shipped
detector modules — every check's category, its `covers` ruling, its registry ids,
its tier — and fails if the record describes a detector suite this repository no
longer has. A check added, renamed, removed or reclassified breaks the build
instead of silently invalidating the table above. It also re-checks the record's
arithmetic against itself, and asserts that the three universal checks are
exactly the non-covering ones this document claims they are.

What that guard cannot catch is a firing count going stale because a detector's
*logic* changed while its identity did not. Re-record whenever it does.
